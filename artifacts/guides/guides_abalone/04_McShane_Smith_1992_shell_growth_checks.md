# Shell Growth Checks Are Unreliable Indicators of Age of the Abalone Haliotis rubra (Mollusca: Gastropoda)

Paul E. McShane (A) and Murray G. Smith

Australian Journal of Marine and Freshwater Research, 1992, 43, 1215-1219. Short Communication.

[p. 1215]

Marine Science Laboratories, Fisheries Division, Ministry for Conservation and Environment, PO Box 114, Queenscliff, Vic. 3225, Australia.
(A) Present address [P. E. McShane]: Fisheries Research Division, Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries, PO Box 297, Wellington, New Zealand.

## Abstract

The results of tag-recapture studies on three Victorian populations of the abalone Haliotis rubra showed that the frequency of growth checks in the shells of individuals was not annual. The use of shell growth checks as a method for age determination of H. rubra is not reliable because of variation in the frequency of growth checks between populations and the inability to count growth checks in the shells of most individuals.

## Introduction

Growth checks appearing on the shell surface of abalone (Haliotis spp.) have been used to estimate age in various species, including H. tuberculata (Forster 1967; Hayashi 1980), H. discus discus (Kojima et al. 1977), H. discus hannai (Saito 1981) and H. diversicolor diversicolor (Kim and Cheung 1985). These studies, validated by independent methods (e.g. tag-recapture), revealed that shell growth checks are deposited annually; age can thus be estimated by counts of growth checks. Muñoz-Lopez (1976) showed that conchiolin was deposited on the inner shell surface of H. corrugata and H. fulgens during winter and that aragonite was formed in summer, resulting in a visible annual growth check. Prince et al. (1988) showed that such internal growth checks in a Tasmanian population of H. rubra were annual. In contrast, Shepherd (personal communication) found that an average of two growth checks were formed each year in four populations of H. laevigata.

In the present study, we compare the frequency of shell growth checks with the incremental growth measured in tag-recapture studies of three Victorian populations of H. rubra. We show that shell growth checks are not necessarily annual and therefore can not be used as reliable indicators of age in this species of abalone.

## Materials and Methods

Studies were undertaken at Sandpatch (37°44'S, 149°36'E), Tullaburga Island (37°44'S, 149°51'E), and Port Fairy (38°10'S, 142°30'E), where random samples of H. rubra were taken. At all sites, care was taken to sample the whole population. Thus, where possible, boulders were overturned and cracks and crevices were searched for small cryptic individuals (McShane et al. 1988a). To estimate growth, individuals of H. rubra were tagged. Details of the tagging study at Tullaburga Island are provided in McShane et al. (1988b). Individuals of H. rubra were tagged at Sandpatch during a 3-year period (1985-87) and at Port Fairy during June 1986. At each locality where these individuals were collected, tagged and measured for length, the time of emersion was always less than 1 h. Small polyethylene tags (6 mm diameter; Hallprint Pty Ltd, South Australia) were affixed to shells with cyanoacrylate 'superglue'. At Tullaburga Island, numbered plastic tags (22 × 12 mm) were attached with rapid-setting epoxy glue (McShane et al. 1988b).

[p. 1216]

Growth increments were determined by periodic recoveries of tagged H. rubra. Where possible, remeasurement of shell length was undertaken in situ with vernier calipers. In this way, tagged H. rubra could be measured (to the nearest millimetre) without being removed from the substratum. In cases where removal of tagged H. rubra was unavoidable, such individuals were not replaced. Growth increments recorded for tagged individuals were fitted to a von Bertalanffy growth equation:

L = L∞ {1 − e^(−K(t − t0))}

The parameters L∞, K and t0 were estimated according to the method of Fabens (1965) by means of the computer program FABGROW (Saila et al. 1988). To estimate t0, we assumed that H. rubra were 6 mm long at age 0.1 years (Garland et al. 1985; McShane and Smith 1991).

All tagged individuals recovered from each population were retained for examination of shell growth checks. A random sample of untagged individuals was also taken from each of the three localities for counts of shell growth checks.

The spire of the shell was ground with emery paper until a small (~2 mm) hole appeared (Muñoz-Lopez 1976; Prince et al. 1988). In this way, successive layers of nacre deposited on the spire were exposed, appearing as concentric rings. The application of several drops of concentrated HCl on the polished surface (the acid was allowed to stand for approximately 10 s prior to rinsing with fresh water) improved the definition of growth checks, as did staining with rose bengal. Only prominent major checks (see Prince et al. 1988) were counted on each shell. The length of each shell (longest dimension) was measured to the nearest millimetre. For each population, the predicted annual incremental growth estimated from counts of shell growth checks was compared with that measured from tagged individuals (increments adjusted to annual increments pro rata to time at liberty).

## Results

The time for which tagged individuals were at liberty ranged between 138 and 1119 days (Sandpatch), 79 and 3980 days (Tullaburga Island), and 517 days (Port Fairy). The length of tagged H. rubra recaptured ranged from 33 to 131 mm. The total number recovered was 75 (Sandpatch), 136 (Tullaburga Island), and 63 (Port Fairy); in all cases, the total recovered was less than 10% of the number tagged.

Age determination of H. rubra by the method of Muñoz-Lopez (1976) was possible only with shells having relatively clean surfaces. The boring sponge (Cliona spp.), when present, eroded the shell of H. rubra such that nacre was lost from the spire. Invasion of abalone by Cliona spp. is common (Forster 1967; Shepherd 1973; Shepherd and Breen 1992) and precluded the examination of many shells of H. rubra. Further damage to shells, preventing counts of shell growth checks, was caused by spionid polychaetes, Polydora spp. (Blake and Evans 1973; Shepherd 1973). Of a sample of 268 shells from Tullaburga Island, only 84 could be used for growth-check analysis. At Sandpatch, 59 of the 128 shells collected were useful for growth-check counts, but none from Port Fairy could be used.

The maximum number of shell growth checks that we counted for any individual of H. rubra was six, compared with the maximum of seven counted for a Tasmanian population of H. rubra (Prince et al. 1988). Shell length varied linearly with counts of shell growth checks ('age') for both the Sandpatch and the Tullaburga Island populations of H. rubra (Fig. 1). Data from tag-recapture studies fitted a von Bertalanffy relationship of shell length with age (Fig. 1; Table 1). The results show that although populations from Sandpatch and Tullaburga Island had similar rates of growth (K), individuals of H. rubra from Port Fairy had relatively slow rates of growth (Table 1).

Fig. 1 shows that tag-recapture data and counts of shell growth checks provided different estimates of growth of H. rubra. These findings are reinforced by a comparison of incremental growth measured for tagged individuals and that estimated from counts of shell growth checks (Fig. 2). Counts of shell growth checks gave estimates of incremental growth that were significantly different from those provided by tag-recapture data. Analysis of covariance showed that this was true for both populations (Sandpatch: F = 65.8, d.f. = 1,77, P < 0.001; Tullaburga Island: F = 7.8, d.f. = 1,136, P = 0.006). Assuming that the frequency of shell growth checks is annual, the results suggest that counts of shell growth checks [p. 1217] underestimate the growth of H. rubra from the Sandpatch population and overestimate the growth of large individuals (>80 mm shell length) from Tullaburga Island. It is noteworthy that the shell-growth-check data suggest linear growth (Fig. 1), with little decrease in incremental growth with shell length (Fig. 2). This may be due to the counting of shell growth checks only for comparatively young individuals, counts for older individuals having been precluded because of shell damage.

## Discussion

Our tag-recapture results provide accurate estimates of the growth of H. rubra and provide a basis for comparison with similar estimates provided by counts of shell growth checks. By tagging a large size range of H. rubra, we reduced the bias in our growth estimates caused by measuring only large individuals (Yamaguchi 1975). Although some tagging methods can retard the growth of H. rubra (McShane et al. 1988b), the use of the glued tags is comparatively benign (McShane 1990). Furthermore, we remeasured tagged individuals in situ without disturbance and took measurements of incremental growth after more than 500 days at liberty in most cases.

The numbers of growth checks present in shells of H. rubra increased with shell length, but comparison with length/age data from the tagging studies conducted at Sandpatch and Tullaburga Island revealed that the checks were not annual, in contrast to the situation shown for a Tasmanian population of H. rubra (Prince et al. 1988). Cessation of activity in molluscs can cause an identifiable growth check in the shell—growth checks often occur during winter (Forster 1967; Poore 1972). Many environmental and endogenous factors influence activity in molluscs, and such factors do not necessarily act regularly, even on annual cycles (Bayne and Newell 1983; Wilbur and Saleuddin 1983). Thus, the number of growth checks per year can vary from population to population, just as reproductive activity, movement and growth rate do.

Our results suggest that the general assumption of annual growth checks in shells of H. rubra is invalid. A further problem is that only 'clean' shells could be used reliably for growth-check analysis. The growth characteristics of individuals with such shells might not be representative of the general population. For example, at Sandpatch and Tullaburga Island, clean-shelled individuals were typically located under boulders and in other cryptic habitats where, presumably, the prevalence of fouling organisms is less. In the slow-growing population of H. rubra at Port Fairy, all of the emergent H. rubra were affected by borers and none could be used for counts of growth checks. If our findings are typical of the species, only fast-growing individuals from habitats with low borer prevalence would be included in shell-growth-check studies, causing obvious bias. In any case, validation of the shell-growth-check technique is required for individual populations (Beamish and McFarlane 1983; Prince et al. 1988); this largely defeats the utility of the technique.
